Part I

What this system is

Before any code: the problem, the exam, the one principle everything else follows from, and the single design idea that makes the app different from a notes app with a newspaper attached.

1. The problem it solves

A newspaper is organised by the day. An exam is organised by the syllabus. Every hour a candidate spends converting the first into the second is an hour not spent learning, and it is the hour almost nobody sustains for nine months.

The daily current-affairs habit fails in a specific, predictable way. A candidate reads the paper, underlines things, and produces a pile of dated notes. Eight months later the pile is unusable: it is filed by date, and the question paper does not ask about 21 August. It asks about the Finance Commission — and the four separate days on which the Finance Commission appeared are four separate pages in four separate notebooks, with no thread between them.

Worse, the same candidate is usually preparing for two exams with two different published syllabi. The same event belongs to different units in each, and reading the paper twice — once per syllabus — is the advice everyone gives and nobody follows.

This system takes the conversion work off the candidate. The admin uploads the day's paper as a PDF; the system extracts every article, scores each one against the actual APPSC syllabus, drafts the survivors into exam-shaped notes, writes questions in the formats APPSC actually uses, and files everything against syllabus units and persistent topics — so that the fourth Finance Commission story attaches to the first three instead of starting a new page.

The claim, stated plainly

The competitive advantage is not OCR and not AI note generation. Both are commodities. The advantage is the chain:

Current Event → APPSC Relevance → Syllabus Topic → Blueprint Keyword → PYQ Pattern → Cross-Paper Connection → AP-Specific Link → Correct Exam Output.

The decision that shapes this whole document

Serving the written paper as well meant carrying a second output shape through every layer: an eight-section note per item, four capture banks, essay questions, a seven-dimension tagging scheme, 54 descriptive syllabus units and a 252-question descriptive past-paper corpus — none of which a ticked paper uses.

All of it was removed on 23 August 2026. What remains is one intelligence process feeding two objective syllabi. Section 33 records exactly what went, so the decision can be re-read rather than re-derived.

This removes Group‑I Mains. It does not remove Group‑I: Prelims is served, and its 27 units are more than half the syllabus map.

Why question generation is the main output, not a bolt-on

Every paper is objective, so the memorise-this block and the questions written from it are not a lane — they are the product. The number of questions an item gets follows how many distinct syllabus units the article feeds, from a base of 4 up to a cap of 10, rather than a flat four per item.

Why Andhra Pradesh is a separate axis, not a subject

AP content runs through every paper rather than sitting in one unit. So the system treats AP as a dimension that cuts across everything: articles carry an ap flag, the relevance score gives AP its own 20 of 100, and the four content buckets include ap as a peer of national. Of the 119 items currently published, 45 are AP — 38%.

The strongest single signal that a story is an AP story turns out not to be its text but its dateline. A report filed from AMALAPURAM is an AP story even when the body never names the State, which is why np_articles.dateline is a first-class column and why a bug that once wiped it was treated as a data-loss incident rather than a cosmetic one.

3. The core principle

ONE SOURCE → ONE INTELLIGENCE PROCESS → MANY EXAM OUTPUTS

NEWSPAPER
  → ARTICLE EXTRACTION
  → FACT + EVENT UNDERSTANDING
  → MASTER APPSC KNOWLEDGE OBJECT
  → SYLLABUS / PYQ / KEYWORD MAPPING
  → MULTIPLE EXAM-SPECIFIC OUTPUTS

The newspaper is processed once. The same processed knowledge then serves both syllabi. There is deliberately no "Newspaper → Group I" pipeline sitting beside a "Newspaper → Group II" pipeline, because that duplicates the expensive part and guarantees the two will disagree.

This principle has a concrete architectural consequence that recurs throughout the codebase: shared libraries, not parallel implementations. The PDF path used by the admin upload screen and the path used by the command line are the same function (lib/ingest.js). The drafting insert used by the newspaper lane and by the older web lane is the same function (insertDrafted in lib/draft.js). Where a second copy was tempting, the reasoning written into the source is always the same: two copies drift, and the drift is silent.

4. Dual routing — one reading, two published syllabi

The atomic object is one item, routed to both syllabi at once. A student reads a day's items through a lens they switch in the header. Read once, file twice.

What the lens means now

It used to switch between a written paper and a ticked one — two answer shapes. Both exams are ticked now, so it switches between two syllabi. The facts are the same; what differs is where on each published syllabus the item sits.

Three rules the code actually enforces

1. No facts, no item. An item with no memorise-this block is an item no question can be written from, and every paper here is answered by ticking a box. The drafter turns it away before spending anything more on it, and the review queue flags it in red. (This replaced the old "no angle, no Group-I lane" rule, which guarded the written paper.)

2. Discard is a first-class outcome. Most news should be discarded. Discards are kept as rows with their reason, and the admin dashboard shows the count as a positive signal. Currently 36 of 155 drafted items and 64 of 411 articles are discarded rows, not deletions.

3. Facts go stale, so say when they were true. Every question carries fact_as_of, every explanation is required, and a seeded ref_corrections register is both injected into the drafting prompt and checked against every draft. A wrong current-affairs fact cannot be caught against a textbook, which is why this gets three layers rather than one.

Part II

The process, end to end

Follow one uploaded PDF all the way to a student's screen. Eight stages, five of which involve no model at all, and two of which cost money. This is the part to understand first — everything in Parts III and IV is machinery in service of it.

5. The journey of one PDF

ADMIN UPLOADS THE HINDU (VIJAYAWADA), 21 AUG 2026, 28 PAGES, ~120 MB

1  EXTRACT      no model  → text with bounding boxes; OCR only where there is no text layer
2  SEGMENT      no model  → blocks become articles; genre read from the paper's own running head
3  SCORE        no model  → 0–100 on five weighted factors, with the breakdown stored
4  SELECT       no model  → rank by syllabus leverage; decide what is worth drafting
5  DRAFT        model    → the master knowledge object: notes, static notes, the facts block
6  QUESTIONS    model    → MCQs in the formats APPSC has actually used for that angle
7  REVIEW       a person  → the review queue; sources, tags and correction hits shown inline
8  PUBLISH      a person  → the day goes live; students see it

Nothing between stage 1 and stage 8 reaches a student. Publishing is always manual.

Where the money goes, and where it does not

Five of the eight stages are deterministic code. That is a deliberate cost and trust decision, not an accident of implementation:

  • Free stages are repeatable. Re-scoring an edition after improving the vocabulary costs nothing, so the vocabulary can be improved often.
  • Free stages are explainable. "Why did this article drop out of today's list?" has an answer — the stored breakdown — rather than a shrug.
  • Free stages are stable. A model asked to score relevance gives a different answer to the same article on Tuesday.
  • Paid stages do what only a model can — write the note prose and the standing material underneath it.

The practical consequence: you can inspect the entire selection for an edition, for free, in under a second, before spending anything:

node server/scripts/draft-articles.js 4 --plan

--plan prints what would be drafted and stops. It is distinct from --dry-run, which drafts everything and declines to save it — same money, same twenty minutes.

6. Stages 1–2 — extraction and segmentation

Extraction

A Python helper (content-pipeline/np-daily/layout.py) reads the PDF and returns text with bounding boxes. Position is the whole point: a newspaper page is a layout problem, not a text problem, and a plain text dump interleaves columns into nonsense. Pages that already carry a text layer are read directly; only pages without one are rasterised and OCR'd, which keeps a 28-page edition affordable in time. The counts of OCR'd and skipped pages are stored on the edition row, because a run that silently OCR'd everything looks identical to one that did not.

The upload itself is idempotent: an edition is identified by publication + date + a SHA-256 of the file bytes, so uploading the same PDF twice returns the existing edition rather than creating a second one. Processing is the expensive part; a double-clicked upload button should not cost two OCR passes.

Segmentation — one rule, and no column grid

The segmentation rule

A body block belongs to the nearest headline above it whose horizontal span covers the block's centre.

That works because of how pages are designed rather than by luck. A headline placed in a column is exactly the mark that the story above it ended there. A six-column headline owns six columns of text; a one-column headline owns one. The rule was checked by hand against all twelve ambiguous blocks on a real page that sets its upper half and its lower strip on different column pitches — the case where a naive column grid fails.

Genre comes from the paper, not from a classifier

The Hindu names its own pages in the running head, so "Editorial", "Opinion" and the PARLEY and NOTEBOOK kickers are read directly off the page. This matters because the same sentence is a fact in a report and a claim in an op-ed. An item drafted from signed opinion gets a different prompt, a forced needs_verify flag, and the author's name carried onto the page.

Same-event merging

Two reports of one event become one enriched item. A duplicate is not deleted — it points at the article it duplicates via merged_into, so a wrong merge can be inspected and undone. Jump continuations ("continued on page 12") are folded into the story they continue. Currently 14 of 411 articles are marked duplicate.

7. Stage 3 — the relevance score

Every article gets a 0–100 score with the breakdown stored alongside it. Five weighted factors:

FactorMeasuresWeightHow it is earned
ASyllabus relevance30Connects to a known master topic, or to recognisable subject territory, or to a syllabus unit. A Tier‑1 topic tops the factor out.
BPYQ keyword match20Matches a blueprint angle the commission has actually asked before — the count comes from the PYQ corpus, not from a hunch.
CAndhra Pradesh20Full marks for an AP story (the dateline counts); half marks where a topic is AP-specific but the article reads as national.
DCurrent importance15Does it name a findable official act? The difference between a decision that can be cited and a meeting that was held.
ECross-paper reuse15How many distinct objective papers the matched topics serve — study once, tick it off in three places. Re-based 23 Aug 2026; see below.

Bands: 80–100 CRITICAL · 60–79 HIGH · 40–59 MEDIUM · below 40 LOW.

Why every score carries its breakdown

Because a number nobody can decompose is a number nobody trusts. The first question anyone asks about "62 / HIGH" is which part came from where, and if that cannot be answered the score gets ignored and the list goes back to being read top to bottom. So score() returns the five components and the reason each one fired, and the caller stores them in np_articles.breakdown. It also makes the weights testable: 30 for syllabus against 20 for AP is an opinion about this exam, and an opinion that can be re-run over past articles can be corrected.

Factor E was load-bearing, and nearly went silently

It read topic_evidence — 149 rows holding one person's reading of the Group‑I Mains papers, and nothing else. Removing the Mains layer would have taken 15 of the 100 points off every article, shifting the whole ranking, with no line anywhere saying why.

It is now measured from this app's own evidence: topic_units joined to ref_units, counting the distinct objective papers a topic's units belong to. It answers the same question the blueprint was being asked — "does studying this pay in more than one place?" — from data that can be rebuilt rather than from a document.

It now reaches further than the blueprint did: 26 articles earned Factor E before, 75 do now.

The thresholds were rescaled with it. Five Mains papers became ten objective ones, so leaving 4+ → 15 would have quietly redefined full marks from 80% breadth to 40%. Now 6+ → 15, 4–5 → 12, 3 → 8, 2 → 4. Measured effect is small and worth stating plainly: mean score 31.7 → 31.3 across 411 articles, three articles leaving the 70+ band. It was done anyway, because a threshold that no longer means what its comment says is how a score stops being trusted.

The veto comes first

Why some articles are excluded before scoring, not penalised during it

A robbery in Nandyal mentions an AP district, names a police officer, and matches the Arrested angle: it would score in the forties on the factors alone. So categories that are never examinable are excluded before any scoring happens, rather than being outvoted by it.

The veto list covers letters to the editor, the archive columns ("A HUNDRED YEARS AGO", "FROM THE ARCHIVES" — reprints of copy a century old that read as perfectly plausible business reporting), crime reports, and sport. Sport is tested separately because the rule is narrower: a match report is not examinable, but doping, sports governance, a major tournament or a sports policy question is. Omitting that check once let a CBSE archery tournament reach 62/HIGH.

8. Stage 4 — adaptive selection by syllabus leverage

This stage decides which scored articles are worth paying to draft.

What was wrong with a flat threshold

The original rule was score >= 45. Because the score is a blend of five factors, an article can clear the bar on AP place-names and importance alone while feeding no syllabus unit at all — and an article that plainly belongs to a unit can miss the bar because nothing else about it scored. Measured across 248 scored articles at the time:

Under the flat ruleArticlesOutcome
Scored 45+ and feed no syllabus unit10Drafted anyway — paid for, and unfileable
Scored under 45 and do feed a unit54Never drafted — the expensive miss

Wrong in both directions, and the second number is the costly one. "Centre notifies key scheme to manufacture mobile phones" scored 43 and feeds four units. "Ethanol blending policy is behind rise in sugar prices" scored 39 and feeds six.

The lever

The number of distinct syllabus units an article feeds is the signal the composite buries. In the 35–44 band, 9 of the 20 articles that match at all match three or more units. An article examinable in three places is examinable, whatever its composite came to.

leverage  = 40 (1 unit) | 62 (2) | 76 (3) | 85 (4+)
            + 15 if a unit is named in the HEADLINE
            capped at 100

rank      = 0.55 × leverage  +  0.45 × composite score

Two details carry judgement. Leverage flattens after four units, because the difference between four and seven is not "nearly twice as examinable" — past a point, a long unit list is a story touching many things lightly, which is the shape of a default block rather than a strong match. And headline placement beats a fourth unit, because a unit named in the headline is what the story is about, not something it mentions.

The guards, each from something the data actually did

The judgement call: no syllabus connection, no draft

Why a score threshold for unmatched articles cannot work

The rule started as "draft an unmatched article if it scores well enough". It was abandoned, because the score is precisely what cannot separate these two:

70  Cultural diversity highlight of gala dinner in Vizag
55  ₹7,470 cr. cleared for infra works in ULBs under HAM

The first is filler. The second is real AP infrastructure. Both score highly for the same reasons — AP place names, money, officialdom — and neither connects to the syllabus. Any cut-off that admits the second admits the first.

So the rule stops guessing. The valuable ones are not lost: they are unmatched because the syllabus vocabulary has a gap, and every run prints them as a to-do list — 17 such articles exist right now. Add the missing alias, re-seed, re-score, and they come back through the front door. Hand-picking with --article ID,ID remains available for genuine exceptions, where the gate is a person rather than a rule.

9. Stage 5 — drafting the master knowledge object

This is the first stage that calls a model, and the design question is not "what prompt" but what the model is and is not asked.

The rule that shapes the whole prompt

The model is not asked to re-derive the bucket, the keyword angles, the topics, or the syllabus units. Stages 2–4 already computed those deterministically and reproducibly. They are supplied to it as findings, not questions. Asking a model a question already settled gets you a second, disagreeing answer.

It is asked for exactly what a scoring function cannot produce: the note prose, the standing material underneath it, and the memorise-this block of facts that questions are later written from.

The prompt got a third smaller when the Mains layer went

The 6,250-token block that is simply gone

The model used to be handed the whole descriptive unit vocabulary to choose from — the largest single block in every paid call. It is no longer asked for units at all, because the objective ones were already matched from the newspaper's own words.

That also removes a contradiction which produced the worst tagging in the corpus. Told nothing, the model returned fourteen units for one story, half of them objective codes copied back from the findings; told not to copy them, it returned three objective codes and no descriptive ones. A prompt saying one thing in its instructions and the opposite in its vocabulary — and the vocabulary wins. There is no vocabulary to contradict now.

Print items carry no invented URL. Question generation runs in the same worker, sharing the corrections guard, the validators and a corpus-wide duplicate hash.

The format mix is driven by evidence, not a rotation

APPSC does not test every topic the same way. A Committee is asked as direct recall and list-matching; a constitutional Article as assertion-reason; a chronology as sequencing. The only way to know which is to count what the commission has actually done. plannedFormatsFor() asks: given this blueprint keyword, which formats has APPSC really used, and how often? Generation follows the observed distribution; the fixed rotation is only a fallback where there is no evidence.

The bug worth remembering

This engine was inert for weeks and nothing looked wrong. plannedFormatsFor sat at module scope and called a logging helper defined inside main(). So whenever the PYQ layer did have evidence, the log line threw a ReferenceError into its own catch, and the catch returned the rotation. The fallback is a legitimate answer, so every run looked healthy.

Measured on the live database: the keyword Scheme has 52 questions of usable evidence asking for [direct_recall × 3, negative_statement]. Every item ever generated had silently received the generic rotation instead.

How many questions an item gets

Not a flat four. The count follows the number of distinct syllabus units the article feeds, from a base of 4 up to a cap of 10. An article examinable in six places deserves more questions than one examinable in one, and that is a fact the system already knows by this stage.

Distractor discipline and staleness

  • Distractors must be real-but-wrong — last year's host, the previous scheme name, the adjacent minister. A distractor that is obviously absurd teaches nothing.
  • Every question carries fact_as_of, so a stale key is identifiable rather than just quietly wrong at revision time.
  • An explanation is mandatory.
  • Questions are deduped on a normalised hash against the whole corpus.
  • A unit code returned with a question is canonicalised against ref_units, and an unresolvable one is reported rather than stored as a tag nothing can query. This is now the only path by which a model-supplied unit code enters the database.

11. Stages 7–8 — review and publication

Everything above produces drafts. The transition to student-visible content is always a human act.

The review queue

Every drafted item lands in Admin → Review queue with its sources, its tags and its correction hits shown inline. That inline placement is a deliberate product decision: a review that requires clicking away to check a URL is a review that quietly stops happening. An item with no prelims_facts is flagged in red, because nothing can be asked about it.

Publishing a day checks first, then publishes

Publishing a whole day validates every draft in it and reports exactly what blocks it, rather than publishing eight of ten and failing silently on two.

Two gates, not one

Why questions have their own status

An item is held by its own status. But questions can be regenerated onto an item that is already live — so the item's status alone no longer answers "may a student see this". ca_mcqs.status is the second gate, and regenerated questions wait under Admin → Review queue → Questions waiting on review until approved. Every student-facing read applies both.

Superseding rather than overwriting

When a redraft replaces an item that was already published, the new item records supersedes pointing at the old one. The history is kept; the student sees the current position. A published item is never withdrawn by a redraft.

Processes

One process serves both the API and the built frontend. There is no horizontal-scaling story and none is needed: the load is a few hundred students reading a page a day.

The two long pipeline stages — processing an edition and drafting from it — are spawned out of process, detached. A ca_runs row acts as the drafting lock, and a run still marked running after two hours is closed automatically on the way past.

A race worth knowing about

The route checks the lock and then spawns the worker; the worker opens its own run row a few seconds later. Every click inside that window passes the check, because no row exists yet. Seven phantom runs were created this way in three minutes when a UI fault made the page go blank and the admin clicked repeatedly. They drafted nothing and cost nothing, but they held the per-edition lock until the two-hour sweep.

17. The syllabus map and its aliases

The insight the whole map rests on

The syllabus says "Distribution of Legislative and Executive Powers between the Union and the States". The newspaper says "Centre-State", "concurrent list", "Article 246". Matching the syllabus's own phrasing would match almost nothing.

So ref_unit_aliases holds 934 newspaper-side phrasings across 52 units — and it is the single highest-leverage file in the repository. Improving it costs nothing and changes what every future article scores.

Unit flags that keep the map honest

FlagEffect
examg1p or g2. This is what the lens switches on, and what Factor E counts distinct papers within.
broadA unit so wide it matches nearly everything — e.g. the 30-mark general current-affairs paper. Excluded from leverage, scoring evidence and coverage reports.
unfeedableA unit no newspaper article can ever feed — mental ability, the language papers. Excluded the same way.

The standing coverage report

syllabus-coverage.js is run after each edition. It answers three questions the map cannot answer about itself: what the map fed, what was never fed, and what over-matched. Currently 110 articles score 45 or more; 93 map to a syllabus unit and 17 map to none, and only three units have never been fed — G1P-A2, G2-P1-U1, G2-P1-U4.

The live concentration problem, and why it is priced rather than fixed

G2-P1-U7 is fed by 61 articles against a median of 7, and 34 of those rest on a single matched term — mostly "Supreme Court" or "High Court" appearing in a story that is really about something else. A unit matching a third of the paper cannot answer "which items feed it", which is the only question it exists to answer.

The report prices the obvious fix rather than applying it, because dropping the weak term strands articles and written questions. Its own advice, and the right reading: a high stranding number usually means the other units are too narrow rather than this one being too broad. Widen those first; it costs nothing already written. This is a judgement call for the product owner, not a bug to be silently patched.

What the Group-I Mains corpus was doing, and why losing it costs little

20 descriptive papers and 252 question slots were seeded here and are now removed. A descriptive paper cannot tell you which FORMAT a topic is asked in, so that half only ever contributed recurrence — and it contributed it to an app that no longer sits the paper. topics.tier is now derived from recurrence in the 1,137 real objective questions instead, which changed the tier of 23 topics.

One seeding decision from that era is worth keeping in mind if a descriptive corpus is ever re-added: one row per question NUMBER, not per alternative. 1 (a) … OR (b) … is one slot a candidate answers once, and counting alternatives separately would double every topic count and inflate recurrence.

19. The topic knowledge graph

119 curated master topics, 441 aliases, 20 curated relations, and a derived reuse map of 182 topic↔unit pairings across 109 item matches.

What it buys

  • Accumulation. The eleventh Polavaram story attaches to the previous ten instead of starting a new page.
  • Cross-paper reuse as a query. "Which papers does this topic serve?" is answered from topic_units, with weight separating a real reuse from one item's stray tag. This is now the source of Factor E.
  • Tier from measured recurrence rather than hand assignment.

Two precision rules that were needed

The matcher originally returned 73 matches where 59 were correct. Two rules fixed it, and both are the same idea as the syllabus-unit evidence rule in §7: a match must be earned — named in the headline, or supported by more than one distinct alias, or carried by a phrase specific enough to stand alone. A single common word is not evidence of what a story is about.

A number that moved for a good reason

Topic matches fell from 152 to 109 when the Mains layer went. That is not a regression: the eight-section Mains note was most of the text being matched against, and a topic is now matched on what the objective lanes actually publish.

The MCQ unlock

Why questions are hidden until the item is marked read

A question answered before the notes teaches the answer, not the topic.

But "mark as read" is a button, and a button takes half a second — the gate records an intention, not a reading. Pacing makes the gate mean what it says: the clock starts server-side when the item is opened, and the questions open when the clock has run.

It is opt-in and stays opt-in, because a discipline imposed on somebody is just an obstacle. A candidate revising the night before wants to move at their own speed through material they have already read; a candidate meeting an item for the first time does not. The same person is both, on different days.

Spaced revision

Five Leitner boxes at 1 / 3 / 7 / 14 / 30 days. A correct answer promotes; a miss drops straight back to box 1. New cards start at box 1 due tomorrow, so they appear as "due soon" rather than retroactively overdue. Dates are local YYYY-MM-DD strings, not UTC — as UTC, the day boundary fell at 05:30 in the morning for every Indian student.

Which jobs are safe to move to a cheap model

Shortlisting is. It is judgement over forty supplied headlines, no recall involved, and a wrong call loses one article the next day's paper will cover anyway.

Drafting is not. It writes the static notes — Articles, sections, landmark cases, AP schemes — from the model's own knowledge, and a cheap model that invents there teaches a candidate something false with total confidence. Measured August 2026: one popular budget model hallucinated on 96% of knowledge questions. Keep drafting on the better model; the whole month costs about $4 either way.

Backup

npm --prefix server run backup          # --verify opens the copy afterwards

Backups use SQLite's online backup API, not a file copy. A cp of a WAL-mode database can silently produce one missing its most recent writes — and it opens cleanly, so you find out during a restore.

Deploy

One box, one process, one SQLite file. systemd with Restart=always, ProtectSystem=strict and ReadWritePaths limited to the data directory; nginx in front terminating TLS with client_max_body_size 130M. X-Forwarded-For is required, not optional: the app sets trust proxy and the login throttle keys on client IP — without it every request looks like 127.0.0.1 and the throttle becomes one shared bucket for the whole internet.

A Windows caveat that matters for this machine

On SIGTERM the process stops accepting connections, lets in-flight requests finish, checkpoints the WAL and closes the database. On Windows that path does not run — Stop-Process and Task Manager both terminate outright rather than signalling.

It is not fatal: SQLite recovers the WAL when the next process opens the file. But it is exactly the window in which a cp-style backup produces a copy missing its most recent writes. On Windows: stop it with Ctrl+C in its own console, and take backups only through server/scripts/backup.js.

28. Invariants the code enforces

  1. No facts, no item. An item nothing can be asked about is turned away in the drafter and flagged in the queue.
  2. Discard is a row, never a deletion. Articles and items alike. A segmenter that quietly loses a column looks identical to one that works, and the only defence is being able to read what it decided.
  3. Every question carries an explanation and a fact_as_of date.
  4. Nothing publishes itself. Two status gates, both human.
  5. Print items carry no invented URL. Citation from the edition row; is_primary = 0; needs_verify = 1.
  6. Derived tables are rebuildable. Any table the matchers produce can be dropped and regenerated.
  7. Scores store their breakdown. A number nobody can decompose is a number nobody trusts.
  8. Shared libraries, not parallel implementations. One ingest, one drafting insert, one model-call implementation, one alias matcher.
  9. The model is never asked what code already settled — not the bucket, not the keywords, not the topics, and now not the syllabus units.
  10. An article with no syllabus connection is never drafted automatically.
  11. Dates are local, not UTC.
  12. Uploads are idempotent on file hash.

The method these have in common

Measure, don't assert. 34. Glossary

TermMeaning
Blueprint keywordA recurring question angle APPSC reuses across years — Appointed, GI tag, Index. Not a fact.
BucketOne of four: international, national, ap, dynamic. Dynamic is the fast-changing edge of another subject — a fresh GI tag, a rate decision.
Composite scoreThe 0–100 relevance number from the five weighted factors.
DatelineWhere a story was filed from. The strongest single signal that a story is an AP story, even when its text never names the State.
Derived tableA table the matchers produce and that can be dropped and rebuilt at any time. The opposite of curated.
Digest / dayOne ca_days row — a calendar day's published items.
EditionOne uploaded newspaper: publication + edition + date + file hash.
GenreNews, editorial, opinion — read from the paper's own running head, not classified.
ItemThe master knowledge object. One ca_items row, routed to both syllabi.
LaneGroup‑I Prelims or Group‑II — the two syllabi one item is routed into.
LensThe student's G1-Prelims / G2 / both toggle. Changes which syllabus units each card shows.
Leverage0–100, from the count of distinct syllabus units an article feeds, plus a bonus if a unit is named in the headline.
Prelims factsThe memorise-this block. The examinable residue of an item, and — since every paper is objective — the whole output.
ProminenceHeadline size as a multiple of body size — the editor's own judgement of importance, free and requiring no model.
